\title{What a Checker Cannot Buy Is the Repair\\
\large A perfect verifier gives a self-refine loop its \emph{worst} score,\\
and actionable repair feedback gives it its best}

\author{Vladimir Rotariu \\ Independent researcher \\
  \texttt{github.com/loopgepa/whystuck}}
\date{July 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A generate--critique--refine loop is usually improved by improving its critique, on the reasoning
that the loop can only repair what it detects. We measure the two halves separately on a
verifiable instruction-following family, with \emph{no} optimizer in the design: every number we
measure scores a fixed, hand-written genome, so nothing here depends on a search, except two
figures quoted from the companion papers. Detection
turns out not to be the binding constraint. Replacing the loop's near-random self-critique
(F$_1 = 0.11$) with the true metric as a \emph{perfect} inference-time gate moves held-out score by
$-0.0027$ ($t = 0.33$ over five replicates): a perfect checker is statistically indistinguishable
from one at chance, and it scores $0.042$ \emph{below} a mere text-statistics tool ($t = 2.89$).
What does pay sits on the fix side. Holding the checker perfect and varying only the repair text,
naming which constraint failed adds $+0.003$ over a bare ``revise'' while a concrete measured edit
adds $+0.0613$ ($t = 8.08$, the best-supported effect we measure), and that configuration beats the
measurement tool outright ($+0.0640$, $t = 5.47$). We also show F$_1$ is the wrong statistic for an
in-loop critique: across a $10\times$ range of task difficulty the same critique's F$_1$ nearly
triples while Youden's $J$ and Matthews' correlation do not improve at all, so a rising F$_1$ on
an easier benchmark is base-rate inflation. The residual is not a diffuse capability wall but one
mechanical skill --- exact word counting, $0.16 \to 0.33 \to 0.50$ under better feedback and never
solved. The practical consequence is a reordering of priorities: do not buy a better detector, buy
a more actionable fixer. Every claim rests on five independent replicates of each configuration,
paired within replicate on shared held-out tasks, and every published claim this paper withdraws is
listed in Appendix~\ref{sec:corrections}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A self-refine loop \citep{madaan2023selfrefine} drafts an answer, criticizes it, and rewrites it if
the criticism complains. Two components can be improved: the \textbf{detector} that decides whether
the draft is acceptable, and the \textbf{fixer} that turns a complaint into a better draft. Almost
all attention goes to the first. The reasoning is intuitive --- a loop cannot repair what it does
not detect --- and the literature supplies a sharp motivating fact: \emph{intrinsic}
self-correction, with no external signal, fails and often degrades, while positive results relied
on oracle stopping signals that ``vanish when oracle labels are not available''
\citep{huang2023cannot}. The natural remedy is a better checker.

This paper measures what a better checker actually buys, by giving the loop a \emph{perfect} one.
The design is deliberately optimizer-free: every configuration scores one fixed genome, so no
result here can be an artifact of a prompt search, of a search budget, or of the wiring that
connects an optimizer to a component. Our contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Detection is necessary and not sufficient} (\S\ref{sec:detector}). Ranked by
    detector quality the three critiques are intrinsic (chance) $<$ tool-fed (precision $1.00$)
    $<$ oracle (perfect by construction); ranked by held-out score they come out $0.765$, $0.837$,
    $0.748$. The best detector gives the worst loop, scoring
    below a measurement tool by $3.04\sigma$ and indistinguishably from a near-random critique.
    The ordering is the evidence, and it is not
    what ``get a better verifier'' predicts.
  \item \textbf{F$_1$ measures the base rate, not the detector} (\S\ref{sec:detector}). Scored
    across three task distributions differing only in difficulty, the same critique's F$_1$ rises
    $0.222 \to 0.357 \to 0.632$ while Youden's $J$ and Matthews' correlation do not improve at all
    and are \emph{exactly} zero at the easiest level. Base-rate-independent statistics are what
    carry a mechanism here.
  \item \textbf{The fix side is where the budget pays, and actionability is the lever}
    (\S\ref{sec:fixside}). Holding detection perfect, more refine attempts are worth $+0.0367$ and
    concrete measured repair instructions $+0.0613$, while naming the failed constraint is worth
    $+0.003$. That resolves the inversion above: the oracle underperformed at one retry because its
    verdicts were terse, not because a perfect detector hurts.
  \item \textbf{The residual is one mechanical skill} (\S\ref{sec:residual}), not a diffuse
    ceiling: under a perfect detector with maximally actionable feedback every ``choice'' constraint
    reaches $1.0$ and the entire remainder is \texttt{exact\_word\_count}.
  \item \textbf{Each claim measured against its own noise, not a borrowed one}
    (\S\ref{sec:curverepeat}). An earlier draft scaled every gap by a $\sigma$ estimated on a
    different genome family; three of six gaps had a margin against the $2\sigma$ line smaller than
    the known spread in $\sigma$. We bought the direct measurement --- each row re-scored five
    times, $90$ runs --- and report paired $t$-tests. Every claim survives; the magnitudes move in
    both directions, three shrinking by $34$ to $52\%$ and the one claim the earlier draft had
    \emph{withdrawn} for lack of power growing by $74\%$ and reinstated.
  \item \textbf{Cross-domain corroboration} (\S\ref{sec:bcb}): on BigCodeBench-Hard the same
    actionability lever is worth $+0.20$, a $3\times$ larger effect, plausibly because a code
    assertion is a sharper repair target than a word count.
\end{enumerate}

A companion paper asks whether a reflective optimizer can \emph{discover} any of this by evolving
the critique. It cannot, and the reason connects to the result here: the optimizer moves the
detector's threshold, and this paper's finding is that thresholds are not where the value is.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Which loop this paper is about.}
During June 2026 industry practice converged on a name for designing the machinery
\emph{around} a model rather than its prompt. Steinberger compressed it into a slogan ---
stop prompting coding agents, design the loops that prompt them
\citep{steinberger2026loops} --- Osmani's essay named the practice \emph{loop engineering}
\citep{osmani2026loop}, and Swyx framed the general form as \emph{loopcraft}, ``the art of
stacking loops'' \citep{swyx2026loopcraft}. Of the four loops that decomposition names
\citep{langchain2026loopcraft}, ours is the \textbf{verification} loop: scoring output against a
rubric and retrying with feedback. The \texttt{ensure\_correct} gene \emph{is} the verifier, the
PASS gate plus \texttt{loop\_max\_retries} \emph{is} the stop rule, and our fix-budget sweep
($R{:}1\!\to\!3$) is a stop-rule experiment. The agent and event-driven loops are out of scope,
since our worker is a fixed generate--critique--refine sequence with no tool-calling agent and is
invoked once per benchmark task, with no triggers, schedules or cross-task memory.

Osmani's central prescription for a verification loop is a \emph{separated} verifier --- ``the
model that wrote the code is way too nice grading its own homework''
\citep{osmani2026loop} --- which is a qualitative claim this design measures directly, and then
sharpens in a direction the prescription does not anticipate: separation is not enough, because the
maximally separated verifier is the true metric itself and it buys nothing measurable.
Chronology: our runs were pre-registered and executed around and before these posts
appeared, so the correspondence is convergent rather than causal --- the practitioner
literature supplied the vocabulary and the stakes, not the hypotheses.

\paragraph{Self-refine and the limits of self-correction.}
Self-Refine \citep{madaan2023selfrefine} popularized generate--critique--refine
with a single model, reporting ${\sim}20\%$ absolute improvement on average across seven tasks.
Huang et al.\ \citep{huang2023cannot} show the sharp boundary: \emph{intrinsic}
self-correction (no external feedback) fails to improve and often degrades
(CommonSenseQA $75.8 \!\to\! 38.1$ after one round, recovering only to $41.8$ after a
second), and prior positive results
relied on \emph{oracle} stopping signals that ``vanish when oracle labels are
not available.'' Self-correction helps only with a genuine external verification
mechanism (a tool, a code executor, unit tests). A parallel line shows LLM
self-verifiers are unreliable detectors: high false-positive rates
\citep{valmeekam2023selfcritique}, and LLM judges favour their own outputs --- a
\emph{self-enhancement} bias \citep{zheng2023judging}. Neither source compares a verifier of one
model family against self-verification, so whether that separation holds is
open; \citet{kamoi2024whencan} survey the conditions
under which self-correction works at all and find that no prior work succeeds with feedback the
model prompts from \emph{itself}, outside tasks exceptionally suited to it --- the cases that do
work rely on reliable external feedback, or on large-scale fine-tuning. The boundary is not
undisputed:
\citet{liu2024intrinsic} report intrinsic self-correction working under narrow conditions
(zero temperature, prompts free of leading bias), which is a condition our worker does not
satisfy --- it samples at the provider's default temperature (\S\ref{sec:setup}), and that
choice is what makes the noise floor of \S\ref{sec:curverepeat} non-zero in the first place.

Our contribution against this line is to take the oracle signal Huang et al.\ identify as the
active ingredient and \emph{supply} it, at inference, as the loop's gate. If reliable external
verification were the binding constraint, that configuration should be the best in the study. It
is the worst of three. What separates our result from theirs is therefore not the presence of an
external signal but its \emph{form}: we vary the repair text while holding the verdict perfect, and
that is where the effect lives. This independently matches
\citet{decomposing2026selfcorrection}'s finding that a model's detection ability does not
predict its correction success, and is consistent with contemporaneous
code-generation findings that the feedback signal and the number of repair attempts --- not
the loop's structure --- drive self-repair gains \citep{feedbackoverform2026,howmanytries2026}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\input{../shared/loop}

\paragraph{No optimizer, and why that matters here.}
Every configuration in this paper is scored on arm~\textbf{A} of the underlying study: a fixed
genome, evolved by nothing. Concretely each run is invoked with \texttt{-{}-arms A}, which scores
the seed genome and runs no search, so no result below can be produced or contaminated by a prompt
optimizer, a search budget, or the wiring that connects one to a component. What varies between
runs is the harness: which signal the critique receives, how many refine rounds it may gate, and
what repair text the refine step is handed.

\paragraph{The two detector interventions.}
(i) A non-oracle \emph{measurement tool}: the critique step is optionally given
\texttt{text\_stats(draft)}, a deterministic one-line readout of measured
properties of the draft (word/character/sentence/bullet counts, longest-word
length, last words). It takes only the text and never sees the task, so it is a
\emph{measurement}, the tool/executor signal \citep{huang2023cannot} found
effective --- not the oracle. (ii) An \emph{oracle critique}: the in-loop gate is the true
held-out metric. This is deliberate oracle contamination, admissible only because it is a
\emph{ceiling} experiment --- we are asking what the loop could do with a perfect detector, and no
claim about deployable performance is drawn from it.

\paragraph{The three repair-feedback levels.}
Holding detection fixed at the perfect oracle, the text handed to the refine step takes one of
three pre-registered forms: \emph{lo}, a bare ``revise'' verdict; \emph{mid}, which constraints
failed with their targets; and \emph{hi}, mid \emph{plus} a concrete \emph{measured} edit per
failed constraint. As actually emitted, for one draft failing one constraint:
\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{lo}\quad \texttt{not all requirements are satisfied; revise}\\
\textbf{mid}\quad \texttt{Failed 1 of 4 requirement(s) --- fix: exact\_word\_count (use exactly 20
words). Satisfied: include\_keyword, word\_max\_length, end\_with.}\\
\textbf{hi}\quad \texttt{- the draft has 15 word(s) but needs exactly 20: add 5 more word(s)}
\end{quote}
All three carry the same verdict and the same detector. Only the actionability differs.

\paragraph{Oracle taxonomy (a required distinction).}
Structural non-oracle-ness (the tool takes no task argument) is necessary but
\textbf{not sufficient}. Our first rule was that a statistic is \emph{informationally} the
oracle when its verifier has no per-task parameter, so the raw measurement alone resolves the
verdict. A pre-spend design review used it to remove \texttt{all\_lowercase} and a comma count,
whose parameter-free verdicts the measurement would simply have been. That rule is too weak,
and we replace it with a three-way distinction:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item a \textbf{scoring oracle}: the held-out metric itself, never admissible in the loop except
as an explicit ceiling experiment;
\item a \textbf{verdict-equivalent measurement}: admissible and deployable, but it settles the
verdict by comparison rather than judgement, because the task statement supplies the target
and the tool supplies the measured value;
\item a \textbf{non-determining measurement}: maps to no verifier, or leaves the verdict open.
\end{itemize}
Every retained field passes the old rule, since each needs a task-supplied target. Under the
new one, four of the eight hard-constraint families are verdict-equivalent once the task
statement is in the prompt: \texttt{words} against \texttt{exact\_word\_count},
\texttt{longest\_word\_letters} against \texttt{word\_max\_length}, \texttt{bullet\_lines}
against \texttt{num\_bullets}, \texttt{last\_words} against \texttt{end\_with}. That is $165$
of $288$ constraint instances ($57\%$) in the hard pool, with at least one on every one of the
$64$ tasks --- though only $2$ tasks ($3\%$) are settled by the tool alone, since
\texttt{forbidden\_word} and \texttt{include\_keyword} are uncovered. This does not contaminate
held-out scores, which never see the tool. What it changes is the reading of the detector
result in \S\ref{sec:detector}: the tool-fed critique's $\mathrm{F}_1$ of $0.11 \to 0.83$ is
substantially the tool \emph{replacing} the detector on the majority of constraint checks,
rather than a prompt that learned to detect better.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Models.} Worker \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} (output cap 320 tokens). No reflection model is
involved in any measurement \emph{we make} here, since none of our configurations optimizes
anything; the reflection field recorded in each run's metadata is the CLI default and is inert.
Two figures in \S\ref{sec:detector} are exceptions, and are quoted rather than measured: the
held-out movements loop-level optimization buys over prompt-only optimization with a substituted
critic, which come from the companion runs of Paper~I and were produced under
\texttt{gpt-5.6-terra} reflection, as were the runs they are compared against.

\paragraph{What is held constant across the curve.} Runs 3, 4 and 7--10 issue byte-identical
first-draft prompts on $54$ calls each --- three seeds of ten held-out and eight training tasks
--- so they score the \emph{same} thirty held-out tasks with the \emph{same} seed gene. Task
difficulty therefore cancels in a between-run difference, and what remains is two independent
draws averaged over thirty tasks. Run~3 is restricted to its first three seeds for these comparisons ($0.7767$ rather than the
five-seed $0.7650$ of Table~\ref{tab:curve}), because seeds~3 and~4 draw different tasks and
would put task-draw variance back in.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We measure the critique as a detector and show it is near-random (\S\ref{sec:detector}), that
making it perfect does not help, and that F$_1$ hides this. We then vary the fix side and find
where the budget pays (\S\ref{sec:fixside}), localize the residual (\S\ref{sec:residual}), and
replace every borrowed noise floor with a directly measured one (\S\ref{sec:curverepeat}). A
cross-domain replication on frontier-hard code follows (\S\ref{sec:bcb}).

\subsection{Detector quality, and the non-monotonic ceiling}
\label{sec:detector}

\paragraph{The critique is an unreliable detector --- measured.} The
mechanism by which a better verify gene \emph{could} help is by catching failures
the generator cannot self-see and steering refinement. We measure the in-loop
critique directly as a binary classifier of ``the draft satisfies all
constraints,'' computed offline from the recorded cassettes (zero cost) by
pairing each critique's \texttt{PASS}/\texttt{FAIL} verdict with the ground-truth
\texttt{mc\_pass} of the exact draft it judged, on the seed loop over the
held-out split (Table~\ref{tab:curve}, left columns). The intrinsic critique is a
\emph{near-random} detector: F$_1 = 0.11$ on run~3 and effectively $0$ on run~5
(it emits \texttt{PASS} on drafts that fail, precision $0.08$). A detector this
poor offers no reliable signal to sharpen.
Strikingly, the \emph{same} critique fed the measurement tool (run~4) becomes a
reliable detector (precision $1.00$, F$_1 = 0.83$) --- which is precisely why the
tool lifts the baseline. This
matches the documented unreliability of LLM self-verification: high
false-positive rates \citep{valmeekam2023selfcritique} and the self-enhancement bias of LLM
judges \citep{zheng2023judging}.

\paragraph{Does a critic from another family detect better? Measured, on three lineages.}
The self-enhancement result invites an obvious remedy --- have a \emph{different} model judge the
draft --- and an earlier version of this paragraph asserted that cross-family verification
outperforms self-verification, on citations that turn out to contain no such finding
(Appendix~\ref{sec:corrections}). We withdrew the claim rather than re-source it, and then
measured it. Scoring the same seed critique as a detector on the same held-out split, with only
the model behind the critique step changed:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccccc}
\toprule
critique model & relation to worker & $n$ & \texttt{PASS} rate & tp & fp & F$_1$ \\
\midrule
\texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} & \emph{is} the worker & $50$ & $0.26$ & $1$ & $12$ & $0.11$ \\
\texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} & same vendor, other lineage & $30$ & $0.13$ & $4$ & $0$ & $\mathbf{1.00}$ \\
\texttt{llama-3.3-70b} & other vendor, other lineage & $80$ & $\mathbf{0.00}$ & $0$ & $0$ & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The ordering is not the one the remedy predicts. Moving off the worker's own weights does help:
the same-vendor, other-lineage critic is the only accurate detector of the three. Moving
\emph{further}, to another vendor entirely, does not help more --- it produces a critic that
\textbf{never emits \texttt{PASS} at all}, missing all nine already-correct drafts in $80$ events.
That is not a better verifier but a degenerate one, behaviourally identical to \emph{always
refine}, and it is the same degeneracy the companion paper on the silent no-op reports the
optimizer converging to. Two caveats hold the reading down: the perfect row rests on $30$ events
with four positives, and every row scores the \emph{seed} rubric, so this measures the model
behind the critique and not an evolved one. The three base rates are comparable: the
already-correct drafts number $5/50$, $4/30$ and $9/80$.

The result matters here because of what it did \emph{not} buy, and seeing that requires keeping
two axes apart. On the \emph{unoptimized} loop, paired on the thirty held-out tasks these runs
share, changing only the critique moves the seed genome's held-out score by $+0.060$ for run~4's
measurement tool, $+0.048$ for the accurate cross-lineage critic, $+0.007$ for the degenerate
cross-provider one and $-0.028$ for the perfect oracle, against a noise floor of
$\sigma(\dd) = 0.029$. On the \emph{optimization} axis, evolving the loop on top of either
substituted critic buys nothing over evolving the prompt alone: $+0.008$ and $+0.014$, both inside
that same noise. Those two are the only optimizer-derived figures in this paper, quoted from
Paper~I rather than measured here (\S\ref{sec:setup}). The two axes answer different questions,
and a number from one is not a comparison for a number from the other.

The first list is the one to resist over-reading. Swapping the critique model is \emph{not} a
detection-only intervention: a different model rewrites the critique's \emph{prose} as well as its
verdict, so it changes what the refine step is told at the same time as what the verdict is --- the
same two-at-once change the measurement tool makes, and the two land within $0.012$ of each other.
The released critiques make the prose difference plain: across every complaint each of them
recorded, the cross-lineage critic answers in a median of $53$ characters (``\emph{add one word;
all other rules pass}'') and the cross-provider one in $210$. So this run
cannot say which half bought the $+0.048$, and we do not claim it does; separating them --- one
model's verdict carried by another model's prose --- is the experiment this result calls for.

What the list does establish is a negative and an ordering. The negative: a judge from somewhere
else is not, on its own, the remedy --- pushed to a second vendor it yields a critic that never
passes, and the seed loop is no better for it. The ordering: the one intervention here that changes
the verdict and \emph{nothing} else is the oracle, whose judgement is perfect and whose feedback
carries nothing to act on, and it is the worst of the four. Detection quality alone does not order
these loops, which is the claim this section makes and the model swaps do not disturb.

\paragraph{$\mathrm{F}_1$ is the wrong statistic, and the $p_0$ calibration shows it.}
Three task distributions differing only in which
constraints the generator may use have measured first-draft pass rates $p_0$ of $0.067$,
$0.267$ and $0.667$, where $p_0$ is the base rate of first drafts that already satisfy every
requirement. Scoring the \emph{same} seed critique as a detector across all three
separates two things that $\mathrm{F}_1$ conflates.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
level & $p_0$ & $\mathrm{F}_1$ & sensitivity & specificity & Youden's $J$ / MCC \\
\midrule
$L_1$ & 0.067 & 0.222 & 0.500 & 0.786 & $+0.286$ / $+0.169$ \\
$L_2$ & 0.267 & 0.357 & 0.625 & 0.318 & $-0.057$ / $-0.053$ \\
$L_3$ & 0.667 & \textbf{0.632} & 0.600 & 0.400 & $\mathbf{0.000}$ / $\mathbf{0.000}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

$\mathrm{F}_1$ nearly triples as the tasks get easier, and it is measuring the base rate, not
the detector: Youden's $J$ and Matthews' correlation --- both of which are zero for a
chance-level detector regardless of base rate --- do not improve at all, and at $L_3$ they are
\emph{exactly} zero because $\mathrm{tp}\cdot\mathrm{tn} = \mathrm{fp}\cdot\mathrm{fn} = 48$.
Across a $10\times$ range of task difficulty $\mathrm{F}_1$ orders the levels and the
base-rate-independent statistics do not: $J$ runs $+0.286$, $-0.057$, $0.000$, which is a fall and
not an ordering. Nor is the one positive value evidence of discrimination --- at $L_1$ only two
held-out drafts are compliant, so sensitivity can take the values $0$, $0.5$ or $1$ and nothing in
between, and $J = +0.286$ is one true positive out of two. What the three levels establish is that
the detector's measured discrimination does not improve as the tasks get easier, while
$\mathrm{F}_1$ nearly triples. This sharpens the claim and bounds its scope:
the $\mathrm{F}_1 \approx 0.11$ reported elsewhere is a figure for one task
distribution, and a reader should not read a higher $\mathrm{F}_1$ on an easier benchmark as a
better detector. It is the base-rate-independent statistics that carry the mechanism.

\paragraph{But detection quality is necessary, not sufficient.} If a poor detector were the \emph{only} obstacle, a better
one should monotonically raise the loop's held-out score. It does not
(Table~\ref{tab:curve}). Read the second row with the taxonomy of
\S\ref{sec:method} in hand: for $57\%$ of constraint instances the tool's readout is
verdict-equivalent given the task statement, so that F$_1$ is largely measurement substituting
for judgement. The intrinsic critique (F$_1\!=\!0.11$) yields seed
$0.765$; the tool-fed critique (F$_1\!=\!0.83$) yields $0.837$; but the
\emph{oracle} critique (F$_1\!=\!1.0$ by construction) yields only $0.748$ --- the
best detector gives the \emph{worst} of the three. The reading rests on the oracle's two gaps,
which differ in strength, and the noise floor sizes them: the oracle sits
$0.088$ below the tool-fed critique at $3.04\sigma$, but only $0.028$ below the
\emph{intrinsic} one at $0.98\sigma$, so on this evidence a perfect checker is
\emph{indistinguishable} from a near-random one and clearly worse than a measurement tool. The
ordering holds on point estimates; the load-bearing half of it is the comparison against the
tool. Both halves have since been re-scored five times each (\S\ref{sec:curverepeat}): the gap
against the tool is $-0.0420$ at $t = 2.89$, replicated, and the gap against the intrinsic
critique is $-0.0027$ at $t = 0.33$. So the reading above is confirmed on a replicated interval
rather than a borrowed floor, with the caveat that both magnitudes are smaller than one draw
suggested. Detection quality is therefore necessary but not sufficient, which independently matches
\citet{decomposing2026selfcorrection}'s finding that a model's detection ability does not
predict its correction success. Two non-exclusive mechanisms explain the inversion,
both pointing past detection: (i) the \emph{fix} step is the true bottleneck ---
even told exactly which constraints fail, the worker cannot reliably repair an exact
word count in one refine round; and (ii) \emph{actionability} matters more than
correctness --- the tool-fed critique emits instructive fix prose where the oracle
emits terse verdicts. Runs 9--10 test (ii) directly (\S\ref{sec:fixside}) and confirm
it, which resolves the tool${>}$oracle inversion as an actionability artefact rather
than evidence that a perfect detector hurts; mechanism (i) survives alongside it,
since even maximally-actionable feedback saturates at $0.873$.

\subsection{Fix-side levers: repair budget and feedback actionability}
\label{sec:fixside}

\paragraph{A perfect detector does not lift the ceiling (run 7).} The oracle-critique
control --- where the in-loop gate is the true metric --- was pre-registered to test
whether the loop \emph{can} convert a perfect detector into higher performance
(expected seed $\ge 0.85$). It cannot: the oracle seed is $0.748$, level with the
intrinsic baseline ($0.765$) and \emph{below} the tool baseline ($0.837$). Detection
quality is therefore \textbf{not} the binding constraint. Even told exactly which
constraints fail, \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} cannot reliably fix hard constraints (e.g.
an exact word count) within the bounded refine budget.

\paragraph{More fix attempts help, but saturate (run 8).} Holding the detector perfect
(oracle) and raising the refine budget $1 \to 3$ lifts the seed $0.748 \to 0.813$: the
\emph{fix} step is a genuine lever. But it still falls short of the pre-registered $0.85$
bar and below the tool baseline ($0.837$) --- a good tool at one retry roughly equals the
oracle at three. The residual is the generator's raw capability: perfect, repeated feedback
cannot push \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} past $\sim\!0.81$ on hard constraints. The gains a
self-refine loop can extract (better detection $+$ more attempts) are real but bounded.
This is consistent with contemporaneous
code-generation findings that the feedback signal and the number of repair attempts --- not
the loop's structure --- drive self-repair gains \citep{feedbackoverform2026,howmanytries2026}.

\paragraph{Feedback \emph{actionability} is a real fix-side lever (runs 9--10).} Runs 7--8 leave the residual fix-step gap ambiguous: is
the worker unable to \emph{act} on good feedback (generator-limited), or is the feedback
itself insufficiently actionable (feedback-limited)? We separate them by holding detection
fixed at the perfect oracle ($R{=}3$) and varying only the repair text handed to the refine
step across the three pre-registered levels of \S\ref{sec:method}. The primary contrast is
the second-largest effect this paper measures by magnitude --- only run 10 over the tool-fed loop is
larger --- and the best-supported by $t$:
$\dd(\text{hi}-\text{lo}) = 0.873 - 0.810 = +0.063$ on the seed held-out (arm A, no
optimizer), clearing the pre-registered $+0.05$ threshold --- the fix step is partly
\textbf{feedback-limited}. All three seeds are positive ($+0.090, +0.025, +0.075$) and an
exact-resample $95\%$ interval is $[+0.025, +0.090]$. We do not lean on that interval:
at $n{=}3$ with three same-signed deltas it \emph{must} exclude zero, so ``excludes zero'' here
is a restatement of sign-unanimity and the sign test is at its $p=0.25$ floor. What does carry it is the magnitude
against the noise floor: $2.18\sigma$, paired on the same thirty held-out tasks
--- and, since that floor was borrowed from another run family, the direct
measurement: $+0.0613$ at $t = 8.08$ over five replicates of each row, the best-supported effect
in the paper (\S\ref{sec:curverepeat}). The whole comparison remains a ceiling result measured with the
true metric gating at inference. Two
sharp qualifications follow. (i) The gain is entirely in the \emph{hi} step: lo $\approx$
mid ($0.810$ vs.\ $0.813$), so naming \emph{which} constraint failed adds nothing over a
bare verdict; only the concrete, measured edit moves the needle. (ii) The lever is the
feedback's \emph{shape}, a hand-built measurement function. Finally, \emph{hi} ($0.873$) is the first oracle
configuration to \emph{exceed} the tool baseline ($0.837$), resolving the run-4 tool
$>$ oracle inversion (\S\ref{sec:detector}) as an actionability effect: the oracle
underperformed the tool at $R{=}1$ because its verdicts were terse, not because a perfect
detector hurts. Yet even maximal, exact repair guidance saturates at $0.873$ (still short of
perfect, no ceiling flag) --- so a generator-limited residual remains. The fix step is thus
\emph{both} feedback-limited (up to $\sim\!+0.06$ from actionability) and, beyond that,
generator-limited.

\paragraph{External feedback lifts the baseline (run 4).} Adding the measurement tool to the
critique step lifts the seed loop by $+0.060$ --- run~3's first three seeds ($0.7767$) to run~4's
($0.8367$), on the shared tasks and not the five-seed figure of Table~\ref{tab:curve}'s left
column --- a direct confirmation of
the external-feedback prediction \citep{huang2023cannot}: the loop pays when the signal comes
from outside the model.
A third \texttt{plan} module (run 5) moved the baseline to $0.757$, i.e.\ by $-0.020$ against the
same three-seed reference, under one $\sigma$ and so no evidence either way: more structure
did not help.

\subsection{The residual is one constraint, not a diffuse ceiling}
\label{sec:residual}

We decompose
the fix-step residual by constraint: replaying each run's cassette (offline, \$0;
\texttt{scripts/\allowbreak analyze\_residual\_\allowbreak by\_constraint.py}), we take the loop's \emph{final}
draft for every held-out task and record the per-constraint satisfaction rate
(Table~\ref{tab:residual}). The result is stark. Under the oracle detector with
maximally-actionable feedback (run 10), every ``choice'' constraint --- \texttt{no\_comma},
\texttt{all\_lowercase}, \texttt{num\_bullets}, \texttt{include\_keyword},
\texttt{end\_with}, \texttt{forbidden\_word} --- is satisfied at rate $1.0$, and
\texttt{word\_max\_length} at $0.90$. The residual is almost entirely
\texttt{exact\_word\_count} (with a small remainder in \texttt{word\_max\_length}):
even told exactly ``the draft has 2 word(s) but needs exactly 20: add 18 more word(s)'' ---
the \emph{hi} format of \S\ref{sec:method} --- \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} hits the exact count only
$\sim\!0.50$ of the time (up from
$0.16$ intrinsic, $0.33$ with which-constraint feedback). This is the known
inability of LLMs to control exact token counts during generation. So the
generator-limited residual is not a broad capability wall --- it is a single mechanical
skill, of a kind a deterministic actuator (not prompt/critique optimization) would

\subsection{The gaps against a cross-run noise floor}
\label{sec:sigmaratios}

Nothing above is interpretable without a noise floor, and this study did not plan one: the worker
runs at the provider's default temperature with one sample per task, so nothing was measured twice
on purpose. It was nonetheless measured by accident. Ten released runs of the companion paper on
the conditional lever
evaluate the \emph{same} unoptimized seed genome on the \emph{same} thirty held-out tasks, so they
are ten independent draws of one fixed number; from their paired differences $\sigma$ for one task
under one draw is $0.1125$, a $\dd$ compares two independent draws so $\sigma(\dd)$ on one task is
$0.1591$, and on a thirty-task mean $\sigma(\dd) = 0.1591/\sqrt{30} = 0.0291$. It applies to the
curve of Table~\ref{tab:curve} \emph{without} a new derivation and without any task-draw term, for
the reason \S\ref{sec:setup} gives: the runs issue byte-identical first-draft prompts, so task
difficulty cancels in a between-run difference and what remains is two independent draws averaged
over thirty tasks.

On that footing the curve's claims mostly strengthen, and one needs narrowing. The external-tool
lift (run~4 over run~3) is $+0.060$ at $2.07\sigma$, and richer repair feedback (run~10 over
run~9) is $+0.063$ at $2.18\sigma$. The result the mechanistic account turns on --- that a
\emph{perfect} inference-time checker does not buy the best loop --- splits into two gaps that
differ in strength, so the ordering alone understates one and overstates the other. Against the
tool-fed loop it is clear:
$-0.088$ at $3.04\sigma$. Against the \emph{intrinsic} loop it is not: $-0.028$ at $0.98\sigma$,
i.e.\ indistinguishable. So the defensible form of the claim is that giving the loop the true
metric as its checker buys nothing measurable over its own near-random self-critique, and is
\emph{worse} than giving it text statistics --- which is the stronger reading anyway, and the one
the residual analysis of \S\ref{sec:residual} explains. Two caveats travel with all of these:
they compare runs recorded on different dates, so they carry the same drift confound as $\sigma$
itself, though the measured drift ($0.007$) is well under the gaps; and runs 7--10 vary retries
and feedback shape as well as checker quality, which \S\ref{sec:fixside} separates.

The next subsection replaces this borrowed denominator with each row's own, and every ratio above
is superseded by a paired $t$-test wherever the two overlap.

\subsection{Measured: the curve's own \texorpdfstring{$\sigma$}{sigma}, and a withdrawal withdrawn}
\label{sec:curverepeat}

Every ratio in the subsection above divides a gap of Table~\ref{tab:curve} by
$\sigma(\dd) = 0.0291$, a cross-run noise estimate recovered at \$0 from ten released runs that
re-score one fixed genome on one fixed held-out set. That estimate was measured on a \emph{seed}
genome at $R{=}1$ in the $p_0$ family, and $\sigma$ is a property of the genome: measured per
genome across that family it spans $5.9\times$, from $0.0064$ to $0.0376$. Three of the ratios
above have a margin against the $2\sigma$ line smaller than that spread: the
tool lift needs only $3\%$ more $\sigma$ to fall under it, actionability $9\%$ and the refine
budget $12\%$. A borrowed denominator was therefore deciding them, so we bought the direct
measurement: each of the six curve rows re-scored $K=5$ times on the thirty held-out tasks they
share, $90$ runs under a priced bound of \$$2.98$. As with the $p_0$ replicates this is arm~A on a fixed genome with no
re-optimization, each replicate carries a salt so record-mode dedup cannot serve one cached draw
$K$ times, and all $90$ replay from their cassettes offline. Unlike the published rows, which were
recorded hours apart, every replicate here was drawn in one session, so these standard errors carry
no provider-side drift.

Four things follow. First, \textbf{the mechanistic claim strengthens}: the perfect oracle sits
below the tool-fed critique by $0.042$ at $t = 2.89$, replicated, where the paper had one draw of
$0.088$ against a borrowed floor. Second, \textbf{the claim we declined to make is now the best
supported in the group}. We reported run 10 as landing ``nominally above'' the tool-fed loop and
refused it at $1.26\sigma$; measured, it is $+0.0640$ at $t = 5.47$. So
\textbf{withdrawal 2 of Appendix~\ref{sec:corrections} is itself withdrawn} --- maximally
actionable repair feedback \emph{does} beat a measurement tool, and refusing the claim was the
right call on the evidence then available and the wrong conclusion about the world. That is the
cost of an unmeasured denominator running in the conservative direction, and it is worth stating
as plainly as the errors that ran the other way: most of this study's arithmetic withdrawals

meant one of the two families was not measuring what it claimed.

\subsection{Cross-domain corroboration: actionable feedback on frontier-hard code}
\label{sec:bcb}

To test whether the actionability result survives a domain where the verify step catches real
\emph{semantic} errors and runs as an executable tool --- the regime
\citet{shinn2023reflexion} find self-correction works --- we replicated it on
\textbf{BigCodeBench-Hard} \citep{zhuo2024bigcodebench}, hard
enough that strong models score far below their full-benchmark pass@1 on it (the Hard
subset is calibrated to stay challenging). An earlier attempt on MBPP+
\citep{liu2023evalplus} saturated its training split, and that failure is what motivated
the move to a frontier-hard benchmark. Each task's \texttt{unittest} methods are partitioned into a visible half
(the in-loop, executable signal) and a disjoint hidden half (the held-out scorer),
reconstructing the anti-oracle split; solutions are graded in a pinned, offline Docker
sandbox (\texttt{-{}-network none}, memory/PID caps, single-thread BLAS).\footnote{Executing
untrusted \emph{model} code at scale surfaces a subtlety absent when only reference solutions
run: a draft can exhaust the container's memory cap. Because we run one capped container at a
time on a host with ample free RAM, such an out-of-memory kill is the \emph{solution's} failure
(scored $0.0$), not a host fault --- distinct from a genuine infrastructure fault (daemon down,
image/mount error), which aborts loudly rather than silently recording a $0.0$.}

\paragraph{Actionable feedback lifts the code baseline too, and more strongly.} The one
intervention that helped on instruction-following --- concrete, measurement-shaped repair
feedback (runs 9--10) --- transfers to code. Holding the arm at A
(no optimizer, so the lever is feedback \emph{shape}) and varying only the in-loop
visible-test feedback between a bare verdict (\emph{lo}) and the verdict plus the source of the
visible tests the solution must satisfy (\emph{hi}), the held-out (hidden-test) score rises by
a mean $+0.20$ across six seeds (per-seed $+0.40, 0.00, +0.20, +0.20, +0.20, +0.20$) --- a
$3\times$-larger effect than the instruction-following actionability lift ($+0.063$), plausibly
because a code assertion is a sharper repair target than a word-count. \textbf{We do not report
an interval for it.} An exact-resample $95\%$ interval computes to $[+0.100, +0.300]$, and an
earlier draft reported that as \emph{excluding zero}; but BigCodeBench-Hard shuffles a fixed
vendored pool, so a seed here selects a partition rather than drawing a fresh sample. At six
seeds of ten the measured held-out overlap is $6.7\%$, which makes both the interval and the
sign test anti-conservative, and our own retraction list withdraws exactly this reading
(Appendix~\ref{sec:corrections}). Nor can we put the effect in units of measurement
noise: $\sigma$ was estimated on instruction-following replicates
and no repeated measurement exists in the code domain, so the $\sigma$
column is unavailable here rather than favourable. What the evidence is, stated plainly: five of
six seeds move by $+0.20$ or more, and on ten binary-scored held-out tasks $+0.20$ is two task
flips per seed. Read it as a large and consistent shift with no calibrated interval, not as a
significant one. One further qualification: the held-out scores carry some
execution non-determinism (the effect size dwarfs it, but we do not over-read the exact value). This reinforces the paper's conclusion:
the productive lever is the fixer and the actionability of its feedback.

\paragraph{Reproducibility scope.} We release cassettes and scores, but do \emph{not} claim
byte-for-byte replay of the full call trace in the code domain: model solutions may use unseeded
randomness, so re-executing them can yield varying test output --- an intrinsic property of
executable-code benchmarks, not of our harness. (The instruction-following runs, which never
execute model code, do replay bit-for-bit.) We normalize the string-level non-determinism we
can: object ids, addresses, and temp paths in failure messages.

\subsection{Cross-worker corroboration: a second worker, twenty seeds, and a prediction we lost}
\label{sec:secondworker}

Every result above runs one worker, \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini}, which \S\ref{sec:threats} lists as this
paper's broadest limitation. We bought the detector comparison again on an unrelated worker:
\texttt{llama-3.3-70b-versatile} served by a different provider, at \textbf{twenty} seeds rather
than three, every cell \texttt{-{}-arms A} so no optimizer runs anywhere. Nothing here extends a
curve above --- a different worker is a different baseline --- so this is corroboration on an
independent stack, reported as such.

We pre-registered the prediction that \emph{the perfect oracle would not be the best cell}, since
that is what the ordering above says. \textbf{We lost that prediction}, and the way we lost it is
the finding. The oracle is the best cell, by $+0.0437$ at $t = 5.80$ over twenty seeds and
$t = 4.70$ over the two hundred held-out tasks paired individually. Taken at face value it reverses
this paper's headline on a second worker.

It does not, and the reason is a confound we had documented one section earlier without applying it
to our own registration. \textbf{The oracle cell is not a detection-only intervention}: it replaces
the verdict with the true metric \emph{and} the repair text with the metric's feedback. It moves
both halves of the loop at once, exactly as a critique-model swap does
(\S\ref{sec:detector}). Holding the gate perfect and varying only the repair text --- the same
\texttt{lo}/\texttt{mid}/\texttt{hi} ladder as \S\ref{sec:fixside} --- separates them:

\noindent
A \emph{perfect verdict carrying no actionable text buys $+0.0110$ at $t = 1.37$}, which is
nothing. The cell that beat the intrinsic loop is the oracle at the \texttt{mid} prose level, so
that is the cell to decompose: of its $+0.0437$, the perfect verdict contributes $+0.0110$ and the
repair text $+0.0327$ ($t = 4.94$), a $25/75$ split. Pushing the prose one level further, to
measured edits, moves it to $+0.0682$ at $t = 9.40$ --- but \texttt{hi} was never the cell under
test, so we decompose \texttt{mid} and report \texttt{hi} as the ladder's next rung rather than
as the effect. Either way the ordering is the same: the verdict is the minority share and never
reaches significance, and the repair text is the majority share at every level of it.

The decomposition itself was \emph{not} pre-registered --- only the prediction it rescues was ---
so we label it exploratory, and note that it reuses the \texttt{lo}/\texttt{mid}/\texttt{hi}
ladder this paper already runs rather than a contrast invented after seeing the result.

That is this paper's thesis, measured on a worker it was not derived from. We do \emph{not} claim
it is measured more strongly here: the original's $t = 8.08$ is over five replicates of a fixed
thirty-task set and carries no task-draw term, while these $t$ values are over twenty seeds
redrawing an of\-ten-overlapping pool, so the two are not a like-for-like comparison and this
paper elsewhere refuses exactly that kind of pooling. The apparent reversal was the bundle, and the
lesson we take from losing the prediction is that a registered prediction about a cell that moves
two things tests neither.

The detector substitutions agree with \S\ref{sec:detector} as well. Neither a smaller
same-lineage critic ($-0.0035$) nor a genuinely cross-lineage one ($+0.0077$) moves the loop.
\texttt{qwen3.6-27b} is the third lineage we have put in the critique seat after
\texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} and \texttt{llama-3.3-70b}, and it is the second to move nothing; the
exception remains \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna}, whose $+0.048$ on the seed loop is real and, as
\S\ref{sec:detector} says, unattributable between its verdict and its prose.

\emph{What those two nulls do and do not license.} Both substituted critics are themselves
degenerate on this worker, and we scored them as detectors rather than assuming: over the $200$
first-critique events, the 8B critic emits \texttt{PASS} once and \texttt{qwen3.6-27b} never,
and \emph{neither ever passes a draft that was already correct} ($\mathrm{tp}=0$ for both,
against $24$ and $20$ already-correct first drafts respectively --- the cells were recorded at
the provider's default temperature, so they do not share a draft set, which is why the paired
tests above pair on seeds and not on tasks). So every non-oracle cell in the table above is a degenerate
detector, and the right reading of the two nulls is \emph{another bad detector does not help},
not \emph{a decent detector does not help}. The claim that a good detector is not the binding
constraint rests here on the oracle rows, where the gate is perfect by construction, and not on
these two.

One further observation belongs to the companion paper on the silent no-op. This worker's
intrinsic critique is a false-\emph{alarm} machine where \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini}'s was a
false-\emph{pass} machine at precision $0.08$: it emits \texttt{PASS} on $1$ of $200$ drafts at
the level the table above uses, and at the relaxed level where $181$ of $200$ first drafts are
already correct it demands a fix on $178$ of them. We give the two levels separately because
they are separate runs; an earlier draft stated a single $1\%$ pass rate for both, which is the
rounding of a three-row table whose rates are in fact $0.5\%$, $1.5\%$ and $1.0\%$. Both critics
are degenerate; neither is a usable detector.

\paragraph{What this run cost us in confidence, and what it bought.} Two defects in our own
instruments surfaced while buying these cells, and both are reported in the companion paper on
the silent no-op as its defects~10 and~11: a \texttt{-{}-temperature} flag that reached the
worker call and not the critique or verdict calls, and a pooled-$\sigma$ estimator that admitted
a run from another worker as a replicate. The first was found here and fixed before these numbers
were recorded; the second never reached a released value. We mention them in this section rather
than only in that one because they were caught \emph{in} this experiment, and a reader entitled
to weigh these results is entitled to know what the instrument was doing while they were taken.

\paragraph{What this does not corroborate.} \emph{The raw cell ranking is inverted between the
two workers}, and no decomposition makes that go away: on \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} the oracle is the
worst of the three critiques and here it is the best. What replicates is the
detection-versus-repair \emph{attribution}, recovered by splitting a cell that moves two things;
the ordering of the cells themselves does not. A reader who wants the ranking to travel should
treat that as unsupported. The two workers also do not face the same difficulty. Under
identical task generation this worker's first-draft pass rate is $p_0 = 0.095$ against
\texttt{gpt-4.1-mini}'s $0.067$, but relaxing one constraint takes it to $0.905$ where the other
reaches $0.267$, so the two stacks are not matched on the axis the companion paper's headline turns
on. Twenty seeds is also not twenty independent task draws: the pool holds $64$ tasks and each seed
draws $18$. We claim a replication of the detection-versus-repair ordering, not of any magnitude.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{Power.} Runs 4--10 use $n{=}3$ seeds (run 3 uses 5). At $n{=}3$ an exact sign test cannot
fall below $p=0.25$ even with unanimous seeds, so no seed-level interval \emph{on those runs} is
significant and we do not present one as such. The exception is
\S\ref{sec:secondworker}, whose cells carry $20$ seeds and do report significant seed-level
$t$ values; that section is a corroboration on a different worker and none of the claims below
rest on it. What follows concerns the $n{=}3$ runs: the actionability effect,
$\dd(\text{hi}-\text{lo})=+0.063$ with interval $[+0.025,+0.090]$, rested on magnitude rather than
replication when first reported --- $+0.063$ at $2.18\sigma$ of the cross-run floor.
What carries every claim now is the five-replicate
paired $t$-test of \S\ref{sec:curverepeat}, whose unit is the replicate rather than the seed.
Those standard errors describe sampling noise on a \emph{fixed} task set, since every replicate
scores the same thirty tasks; they contain no task-draw component and are not a substitute for
more seeds.

\textbf{The oracle configurations are ceiling experiments, not deployable systems.} Runs 7--10
gate the loop on the true held-out metric. That is oracle contamination by construction, and no
absolute score from those rows should be read as achievable performance. What the rows license is
a \emph{comparison} under a fixed, perfect detector, which is exactly what isolates the fix side.

\textbf{Runs 7--10 vary retries and feedback shape as well as checker quality.} The
detector-quality column of Table~\ref{tab:curve} is therefore not a single-variable axis across
all seven rows; \S\ref{sec:fixside} separates the levers, and the $R$ column is printed so no row
is read against a row at a different budget by accident.

\textbf{Between-run comparisons carry provider drift.} The gaps in Table~\ref{tab:curve} compare
runs recorded on different dates, so they carry the same drift confound as the cross-run $\sigma$
that scaled them, though the measured drift ($0.007$) is well under the gaps. The replicates of
\S\ref{sec:curverepeat} were drawn in one session and do not have this problem, which is one more
reason they supersede the ratios.

\textbf{A do-nothing floor in the
metric:} the fraction-of-constraints-satisfied score is not anchored at zero for an empty
answer --- the empty string scores $0.548$ on the vendored IFBench slice and $0.420$ on
generated multi-hard, and five of eleven IFBench verifiers are vacuously satisfied by text
containing no whitespace, quotes or bullets (\texttt{\_sub\_bullets("")} reduces to
\texttt{all([]) = True}). These are faithful ports of the upstream checkers and the floor
applies to every configuration equally, so it does not bias a between-row gap, but it compresses the scale: on IFBench
the floor sits at or above our reported seed baselines. It also makes the metric a poor
instrument for this loop, since a metric that partly rewards saying less is exposed to a
critique that pushes toward shorter answers.

\textbf{Single worker model:} results may be model-dependent \citep{huang2023cannot}. In
particular the residual of \S\ref{sec:residual} is a \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} limitation, and a
worker that counts words reliably would move the ceiling without any change to the loop.
\textbf{One task family:} verifiable instruction-following may be a domain where the critique
has unusually little leverage, which is why \S\ref{sec:bcb} checks the actionability lever
against executable code. \textbf{Held-out generalization:} the
generator's parameter pools are small, so train and held-out tasks can share parameter
combinations; this affects all rows equally but softens the generalization claim.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\textbf{Does the self-refine loop help?} Only through \emph{external} signal --- a measurement tool
($+0.060$, $2.07\sigma$) or more refine attempts ($+0.065$, $2.24\sigma$), both paired on the same
thirty held-out tasks --- never through the model grading its own draft, whose critique is a
near-random detector (F$_1\!=\!0.11$) and whose replacement by a \emph{perfect} checker
moves held-out by $0.98\sigma$, i.e.\ not at all. Those three comparisons have since been
re-scored five times each, and all three survive on a replicated interval rather than a
borrowed noise floor, with smaller magnitudes than one draw suggested
(\S\ref{sec:curverepeat}). The two positive effects survive as effects, at $p<0.05$
($t = 4.38$ and $t = 3.12$); the third survives as a \emph{null}, at $t = 0.33$, which is the
stronger reading and not a significance claim.

The loop's bottleneck is therefore the fixer, not the detector, and the two are cleanly separable by
experiment. Give the loop a \emph{perfect} inference-time checker and held-out score moves
$-0.0027$ ($t = 0.33$) against its own near-random self-critique --- a null confirmed on five
replicates rather than merely unrefuted --- while the same perfect checker scores $0.042$
\emph{below} a deterministic text-statistics tool ($t = 2.89$). Ranked by detector quality the
configurations run intrinsic $<$ tool $<$ oracle; ranked by outcome they run $0.765$, $0.837$,
$0.748$. A monotone relationship between detection and performance was the premise of ``get a
better verifier'', and it is absent.

What resolves the inversion is \emph{actionability}, not correctness. Holding the checker perfect,
a bare ``revise'' yields $0.810$ and naming the failed constraint yields $0.813$: identifying the
failure is worth essentially nothing. A concrete measured edit --- ``the draft has 15 word(s) but
needs exactly 20: add 5 more word(s)'' --- yields $0.873$, worth $+0.0613$ at $t = 8.08$, and beats
the measurement tool outright at $+0.0640$, $t = 5.47$. More refine attempts are worth $+0.0367$.
So the two things that pay are both on the fix side, and the one that pays most is the shape of the
repair instruction.

Two methodological points travel with this. First, \textbf{F$_1$ is the wrong statistic for an
in-loop critique}: across a $10\times$ range of task difficulty the same critique's F$_1$ nearly
triples while Youden's $J$ and Matthews' correlation do not improve at all, so a rising F$_1$ on
an easier benchmark is base-rate inflation and not a better detector. Second,
\textbf{$\sigma$ is a property of the genome, so a borrowed denominator can decide a claim}. Ours
was borrowed from another genome family and three of six gaps had less margin against $2\sigma$
than the known spread in $\sigma$. Measuring it directly cost \$$2.98$ of priced bound, upgraded
every ratio to a five-replicate $t$-test, shrank three magnitudes by $35$ to $52\%$, and
reinstated one claim the earlier draft had withdrawn for lack of power. The borrowing had been
conservative in all six rows, which we could not have known in advance and had in fact guessed
backwards.

Finally, the residual is not a wall. Under a perfect detector with maximally actionable feedback
every choice constraint reaches $1.0$ and the entire remainder is one mechanical skill: exact word
counting, which rises $0.16 \to 0.33 \to 0.50$ with better feedback and never reaches $1$. That
localization is actionable in a way a diffuse ceiling would not be --- a deterministic counter
would likely recover most of it, and no amount of prompt or rubric rewriting did.

We release the harness, every cassette, the pre-registrations and the corrections register. This
paper and its figures are licensed CC BY 4.0 and the accompanying code Apache-2.0, so the
artifact can be redistributed and built on rather than merely read.

\section{Withdrawals, Corrections and Registered Deviations}
\label{sec:corrections}

Earlier drafts of this work published claims it now withdraws. Reporting guidance for
preregistered work asks that deviations be disclosed in
one systematic place rather than left for a reader to reconstruct by comparing documents
\citep{willroth2024bestlaid}, so every such change affecting \emph{this} paper's claims is
collected here and the body text states only the surviving claim. \textbf{Thirteen} entries follow. The
series' other two papers carry the entries bearing on their own claims, and the full
register across all three, with the superseded values themselves, is in the released repository.

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{$\mathrm{F}_1 = 0.11$ as a property of the detector.} It is a figure for one task
    distribution. Across a $10\times$ range of $p_0$ the same critique's $\mathrm{F}_1$ nearly
    triples while Youden's $J$ and Matthews' correlation do not improve at all
    (\S\ref{sec:detector}), so a rising $\mathrm{F}_1$ on an easier benchmark is base-rate
    inflation.
  \item \textbf{``Run~10 beats the tool-fed loop.''} An earlier caption called that gap decisive.
    On its single draw it is $1.26\sigma$, so we withdrew it. \textbf{That withdrawal is itself
    withdrawn}: five replicates of both rows give $+0.0640$ at $t = 5.47$
    (\S\ref{sec:curverepeat}), so the claim stands after all. Both movements are left on the
    record, because a register showing only the claims that were too strong would misrepresent
    which way this paper's errors ran.
  \item \textbf{The oracle-checker ordering.} Published as an ordering only. It splits into two
    gaps of unequal strength: $-0.088$ at $3.04\sigma$ against the tool-fed loop, which carries
    the claim, and $-0.028$ at $0.98\sigma$ against the \emph{intrinsic} loop, which does not.
    Both ratios are stated here in their \emph{post}-correction form, against the curve's own
    $\sigma = 0.0291$ rather than the borrowed denominator entry~4 retired; we say so because a
    register that quietly restates a corrected figure asks the reader to check which vintage
    each entry is written in.
  \item \textbf{Six curve ratios scaled by a borrowed $\sigma$.} Every ratio in
    Table~\ref{tab:curve} divided by a denominator measured on a different genome family, in a
    study that also shows $\sigma$ to be genome-specific across a $5.9\times$ range. Three of the
    six had a margin against $2\sigma$ smaller than that spread. Now measured directly on the
    curve's own rows (\S\ref{sec:curverepeat}): every claim survives and is upgraded from a
    single draw to a five-replicate $t$-test, three magnitudes shrink by $34$ to $52\%$ and one
    grows by $74\%$, all within what one draw accounts for, and the borrowed $\sigma$ turns out to have been conservative for
    all six rows --- which is why entry~2 needed reinstating rather than strengthening.
  \item \textbf{Any BigCodeBench-Hard interval or sign test read as replication across
    independent seeds.} That benchmark shuffles a fixed vendored pool, so a seed selects a
    partition rather than drawing a fresh sample; at six seeds of ten the measured held-out
    overlap is $6.7\%$. The affected effect is reported without an interval
    (\S\ref{sec:bcb}).
  \item \textbf{Cost figures.} The harness records a pre-flight \emph{bound} on spend, not an
    invoice. Bounds are labelled as bounds throughout; an earlier draft
    reported one as a measured spend.
  \item \textbf{The five-replicate $t$-tests used a population standard deviation.} Every
    $K{=}5$ standard error divided the \emph{population} sd of the five draws by $\sqrt{5}$, and
    the result was then compared against critical values from $t(4)$ --- a distribution that
    exists \emph{because} the denominator is $n-1$. The unbiased estimator inflates $\sigma$ by
    $\sqrt{5/4} = 1.118$, so every $t$ in earlier drafts exceeded its corrected value by
    $11.8\%$ (equivalently, correcting one lowers it by $10.6\%$; we state both because the
    bare phrase ``too large by'' does not say which value is the denominator). The same
    mistake in the cross-run estimate, over $30$ paired tasks, \emph{understated} $\sigma(\dd)$
    as $0.0286$ where the unbiased estimator gives $0.0291$, and scaled every ratio built on it
    by $1.7\%$. Both are corrected throughout, and $0.0291$ is the value divided by here. The
    error was invisible to \texttt{verify\_paper\_claims.py}, which checks the paper against the
    same function that computed it, so paper and checker were wrong together --- the same
    shared-error blind spot as the borrowed $\sigma$, one level down.
    No verdict in this paper turns on it: all six comparisons of
    Table~\ref{tab:curvepairs} keep their side of the $2.776$ line, the closest being the
    oracle-under-tool gap at $t = 2.89$. Only the magnitudes of the $t$ statistics move.
  \item \textbf{Four restatements of a corrected statistic, and one selective summary.} A
    paragraph-by-paragraph audit after the estimator correction found this paper quoting the
    oracle-under-intrinsic $t$ as $0.37$ in two places (one of them the abstract) and $0.33$ in two
    others, and the actionability $t$ as $9.03$ in the conclusion against $8.08$ elsewhere; the
    curve-$\sigma$ ratio range also disagreed with the companion paper's. Every pinned check passed
    throughout, because each was anchored to one phrasing of a claim the paper makes four times.
    Separately, ``three magnitudes shrink by $35$ to $52\%$'' was selectively true: a fourth
    magnitude falls to near nothing and a fifth \emph{grows} by $74\%$, so the sentence read as the
    shape of the whole correction when it described part of it. All restatements now agree, and a
    check compares every restatement of a statistic against the first.
  \item \textbf{``Youden's $J$ and Matthews' correlation stay at or below chance.''} Withdrawn as
    stated: at $L_1$ they are $+0.286$ and $+0.169$. What is true, and is the load-bearing point, is
    that they \emph{do not improve} as $\mathrm{F}_1$ nearly triples --- $J$ runs $+0.286$, $-0.057$,
    $0.000$, a fall rather than an ordering. Nor is the positive value evidence of discrimination:
    at $L_1$ two held-out drafts are compliant, so it is one true positive out of two.
  \item \textbf{An external-tool lift that invited the reading its own footnote disowns.} The
    fix-side section reported the tool raising the seed loop ``from $0.765$ to $0.837$'', which
    subtracts to $+0.072$ --- and $0.765$ is run~3's \emph{five}-seed mean, while every gap in this
    paper uses its first three ($0.7767$). The dagger footnote to Table~\ref{tab:curve} says so
    explicitly, so the paper contradicted itself across two pages with both numbers individually
    correct. The lift is $+0.060$.
  \item \textbf{Two results attributed to citations that contain neither.} At two sites this paper
    credited \citet{valmeekam2023selfcritique} --- once jointly with
    \citet{huang2023cannot} --- with ``a self-enhancement bias'' and ``cross-family verifiers
    beating self-verification''. Only the false-positive-rate clause is in either. Valmeekam et
    al.\ use GPT-4 as both generator and verifier against an external \emph{sound} verifier, so
    there is no cross-family LLM comparison, and the phrase ``self-enhancement bias'' does not
    occur; Huang et al.\ study \emph{intrinsic} self-correction and compare no verifier families at
    all. The term originates with \citet{zheng2023judging}, absent from our bibliography until now,
    and is cited for that clause alone. The cross-family clause is \textbf{withdrawn rather than
    re-sourced}. The over-attribution mattered here more than in Paper~I: \S\ref{sec:detector} used
    it to say our detector findings ``match'' documented prior results, and one of the two things it
    claimed to match was not documented anywhere we had read. Found by three of the twelve cold
    reviewers, all in condition~B.
  \item \textbf{Three fragility margins left behind by the estimator correction.}
    \S\ref{sec:curverepeat} said the borrowed-denominator ratios needed ``only $5\%$ more $\sigma$''
    ($11\%$, $14\%$) to fall under the $2\sigma$ line. Those three reproduce only under the
    \emph{population}-sd $\sigma = 0.0286$ the correction retired; under the $\sigma = 0.0291$ this
    paper actually divides by, the margins are $3\%$, $9\%$ and $12\%$. This is the second site
    ``corrected throughout'' missed, after the $1.12$ of Paper~I's register, and the two share a
    signature: a value derived from $\sigma$ rather than printed as $\sigma$, so a search for the
    denominator does not find it. Like the first, it erred in the flattering direction --- it made
    the ratios look \emph{less} fragile than they are, which is the opposite of what the paragraph
    argues, so the corrected figures strengthen the case for having bought the direct measurement.
    All three are now derived from the same \texttt{\_curve\_gap} ratios the surrounding prose
    pins, so a future change of denominator moves them with everything else. Found independently by
    two of the twelve cold reviewers, reported as separate candidates because they quoted different
    halves of the sentence.
  \item \textbf{The second worker's intrinsic \texttt{PASS} rate, given as $1\%$ in two places.}
    It is $0.5\%$: one draft of $200$ at the level \S\ref{sec:secondworker} reports. The
    source of the error is a calibration table printed at two decimal places, where the three
    levels' true rates of $0.005$, $0.015$ and $0.010$ all render as \texttt{0.01}; we read one
    worker-level fact out of a column that had been rounded until three different numbers looked
    like one. A single sentence then combined that rate with a false-alarm count from a
    \emph{different} level. Both are now stated per level (\S\ref{sec:secondworker}).
    \textbf{This one is in the register for how it survived, not for its size.} The rate sat
    inside the regular expression the claim checker uses to \emph{locate} that table row, so the
    numeral was a search key and not a compared value --- the checker read straight past it on
    every one of its runs, and reported all-pass while doing so. A literal used to find a claim
    cannot also test it. Every rate here is now pinned as a value in its own right, so the anchor
    and the assertion move together, and this paper's own thesis --- that a check can report
    agreement about something it never compared --- is the reason the entry exists.
\end{enumerate}
